Switches and VLANs

L2 Switches' rack lane pools, the L3 Switch's segment routes and QoS, and VLAN trunking with access and trunk ports, native segments and the Trunk Port.

Switches are 1U rack devices that do two jobs. They save the network's lanes, by giving the other devices in their rack lanes of their own. And they let a rack's devices work for more than one network segment, which is what VLANs are about. The L3 Switch also moves items between those segments.

Lane pools#

Without a switch, every device in a rack takes its lanes from the network, one lane each for most devices. A busy rack can use a lot of them (see Lanes).

A switch changes that. Its pool gives lanes to the other devices in its rack, in unit order, until the pool is used up. Those pooled devices cost the network nothing; the network only carries the switch's own one-lane uplink. Devices beyond the pool take their lanes from the network as before. Two switches in a rack add their pools together.

  • An L2 Switch drains rack.switchDrain (default 2) FE/t, an L3 Switch rack.l3SwitchDrain (default 3).
  • Switches have High lane priority by default, so the rack keeps their uplinks before anything else.
  • A switch's popup shows its pool's use ("used / capacity"), how many devices it pools, and its uplink.

The switch panel lists the rack's devices: each one's unit, whether it's pooled, its lanes and the segment it serves.

Segments and VLANs#

A Segment Isolator cuts a cable run into two separate networks, called segments. Normally a rack's devices all work for the rack's own network. With a switch, any pooled device can be put on another segment instead: a NAS on segment 2 is storage for that segment's network, not the rack's.

Each segment a rack knows has an ID. The rack's own network is 1.

Linking a segment to a rack#

  1. Sneak-use a Link Card on the half of a Segment Isolator facing the segment you want. The action bar says "Segment on this side stored".
  2. With the rack's front door open, use the card on a switch's unit. The action bar says "Segment linked to the rack".

The rack gives it the next free ID (2, 3, ...). A rack can link up to 5 segments of its own. Using the card on the switch again unlinks the segment: devices on it go back to the rack's own network, and L3 routes through it are removed.

Linking needs Rack access at the rack and Build on the segment's network.

Access and trunk ports#

Each pooled device is a port on the switch, and the panel's Ports view sets how it works:

Port modeServes
Access (the default)One segment: the one picked for it
TrunkIts native segment, plus the allowed segments: all the segments the rack knows, or a list you pick
  • Picking a device's segment: in the device list, click the device's segment to step to the next segment the rack knows. For a trunk, this is its native segment.
  • Switching mode, allowed segments: in the Ports view. These need Build on the rack's network, as trunk settings do. Adding to a trunk's list steps through the segments it doesn't carry yet; "all" goes back to carrying every segment.

A device on a segment works for that segment's network. If that network can't be reached (unloaded, or down), the device has no network: it never falls back to another one. A device that's no longer pooled (the pool shrank) goes back to the rack's own network.

On a Chassis Switch, a port can be set to trunk mode only with a Trunk Card installed.

The Trunk Port#

Linking segments rack by rack gets tedious when you have many racks. A Trunk Port carries segments into a whole network instead: every Server Rack on that network can put its pooled devices on them, as if it had linked them itself.

The Trunk Port is a cable part. Mount it on a cable of the network your racks are on.

  • Adding a segment: store a segment (or a network) on a Link Card, then use the card on the Trunk Port without sneaking. Again takes it off. It carries up to 8 segments, given the IDs 10, 20, 30 and so on (up to 4094).
  • Modes, in its panel: Trunk (the default) carries the allowed segments (all of them, or those you tick) plus the native segment; Access carries only the native segment.
  • The panel has the mode, an allow toggle for each segment, the native segment, and unlink buttons. Opening it and changing it need Build.
  • A Trunk Port only carries segments while it's online. If two Trunk Ports on a network carry the same ID, the first one found has it, and a rack's own linked segment wins over a Trunk Port's with the same ID.
  • Racks list a Trunk Port's segments after their own, so their own segments keep their places in L3 routes.

The panel also shows the lanes that devices on its segments use, against rack.trunkPortLanes (default 64). That figure is a guide only: each device's lanes are granted by its own rack, and nothing limits a Trunk Port's segments to it.

Segments on a trunk still can't see each other's storage. Only an L3 Switch or a Router moves items between them.

The L3 Switch#

The L3 Switch is an L2 Switch with a rack.l3SwitchLanes (default 32)-lane pool that also routes items between the segments its rack knows: the rack's own network, the segments it has linked, and those Trunk Ports carry to it. Its panel has three tabs: Devices, Routes and QoS.

Routes#

An L3 Switch holds up to 8 routes. Each route goes from one segment to another (by ID, so segments coming and going don't point it elsewhere) and is one of two kinds:

  • Move: once a second it moves the items its filter passes from the source segment's storage into the destination's.
  • Share: nothing moves. Instead, the source segment's items that the filter passes become part of the destination's storage, as its terminals and crafting see them, while the switch is online. A Share route's options:
    • Read (see and take out) or Read/Write (put in too, once the destination's own storage is full),
    • whether crafting may use the shared items,
    • one way, or both ways (bidirectional).

Filters. Each route has a 9-entry filter, as a port's is, with its deny, tag and component options. An empty allow list passes nothing, so a new route moves nothing until you set it up; an empty deny list passes everything. Drag items from JEI onto a route's filter box to set it.

Rate. The switch moves up to rack.l3SwitchRate (default 32) items a second in all, shared between its active Move routes in turn.

QoS#

QoS rules decide which items a switch's Move routes deliver first when there's more to move than its rate allows. Up to 8 rules, each an item (or empty, for any item) and a level: High, Normal or Low.

  • An item's level is the highest level of the rules it matches; items no rule matches are Normal.
  • Each Move route moves its High items first, then Normal, then Low.

QoS only orders the items within this switch's own routes. It doesn't affect crafting jobs, ports, other devices or the network's lanes, and Share routes have nothing to order.

Example#

A rack on your main network (segment 1) has an L3 Switch, and you've linked your farm's segment as segment 2. A Move route from 2 to 1 with Wheat and Carrots in its filter brings the harvest home; a QoS rule marking Wheat High makes sure the wheat comes first when the farm outpaces the switch. A Share route from 1 to 2, Read only, lets the farm's terminals see your main storage's seeds without being able to put things in.

Switches and the Terminal OS#

Switches appear in Work with Devices as SWITCH01, L3SWITCH01 and so on, and the Trunk Port as TRK01. RTVLANES reads a network's lane use.
