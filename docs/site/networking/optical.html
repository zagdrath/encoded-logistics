Optical networking

Fiber Cable span loss, the Optical Amplifier and Optical Multiplexer in block and rack form, and the Optical Transport Shelf's channels and 1+1 protection.

On this page

  2. Optical Amplifier
  3. The rack amplifier
  4. Optical Multiplexer
  5. Setting up a pair
  6. Optical Transport Shelf
  7. Watching it from the Terminal OS

Fiber Cable carries a network over long distances, but light fades: a very long run of fibre loses lanes. The optical gear deals with that. Amplifiers regenerate the light so a run can go on, and multiplexers carry several separate networks over a single fibre line between two points.

Fiber Cable and span loss#

Fiber Cable carries lanes.perFiberCable (default 32) lanes, the same as Dense Cable, but it's the only cable that can carry optical lines, and the only one with span loss.

A continuous run of Fiber Cable longer than optical.fiberSpanThreshold (default 256) blocks loses one lane for every further optical.fiberSpanLossStep (default 32) blocks, down to a minimum of 1. With the default settings:

Run lengthLanes lostLanes left
Up to 256 blocks032
300 blocks131
512 blocks824
1,024 blocks248

How it's worked out:

  • A run is a stretch of Fiber Cable blocks, plus any unpowered Optical Amplifiers in it. Anything else ends the run there: a powered amplifier, another kind of cable or block, or a remote link such as a Network Bridge.
  • A run's span is its longest path between its ends. Every link in the run, and every link into it, carries at most the lanes left after its span's loss.
  • Signal quality is 100 % less 4 % for each lane lost.
  • Network Cable, Dense Cable, Bridges and Point-to-Point Links don't lose anything.

Look at a Fiber Cable to see a popup with the network it's on (or Not on a network), its span length since the last amplifier, and the lanes running through it out of those it carries after loss. Turn span loss off altogether with optical.fiberSpanLoss (default true).

Optical Amplifier#

The Optical Amplifier is an inline housing that sits in a Fiber Cable run and regenerates the light.

  • Placing: it lies along the direction you're looking (horizontally). Cables join it only at its two ends: light comes in at the back and leaves at the front.
  • On the network: it passes lanes like a Fiber Cable, takes one lane itself, and drains optical.amplifierDrain (default 1) FE/t.
  • Powered, it ends the span: span loss starts counting again from zero after it. Unpowered, it's just more fibre and the run goes on through it.
  • It's powered while it's online on its network, or while an online Optical Multiplexer or Transport Shelf whose line runs through it powers the line.
  • States: off, on (amplifying), or fault: "input loss of signal", with no fibre at its back.
  • Popup: its input and output lanes, and the spans on either side. Its device name is AMP01 and so on.

Because an amplifier needs a lane, one on a run that has run out of lanes may not get one; then it isn't powered by its network, though a multiplexer's or shelf's line through it still powers it.

The rack amplifier#

The Optical Amplifier (Rack) does the same job for lines that pass through a Server Rack: a multiplexer's or shelf's line can come into the rack at one connection point and leave at another, and its span starts again there. It needs Fiber Cable at two or more of the rack's connection points (its input and output); with fewer it faults with "input loss of signal". While it's unpowered, the rack passes no line through. It's made from the block form (see Rack devices).

Optical Multiplexer#

An Optical Multiplexer carries several separate networks over one fibre line. A pair of them, one at each end of a Fiber Cable line, joins channel 1 at one end to channel 1 at the other, channel 2 to channel 2, and so on. The networks on the channels never mix with each other, and the line itself joins nothing.

  • Placing: it faces you. Its front is the line port: the Fiber Cable there is its line. Every other side joins the network it draws power from, as a device with one lane, draining optical.multiplexerDrain (default 4) FE/t.
  • Channels: optical.multiplexerChannels (default 8) of them (the option goes up to 16).
  • Its device name is MUX01 and so on.

Setting up a pair#

  1. Lay the line: run Fiber Cable from one multiplexer's front to the other's. The line may pass through Optical Amplifiers (along their axis), a Network Bridge pair, and Server Racks with an online rack amplifier. It follows only Fiber Cable: any other cable breaks it.
  2. Pair them: sneak-use a Link Card on one multiplexer, then use it on the other.
  3. Give channels their networks: sneak-use a Link Card on a cable or device of a network (or on a Segment Isolator half, for a segment), then use it on a multiplexer. It goes on the channel selected in the multiplexer's panel, or the first free one; if none is free the action bar says so. Do the same at the other end for the same channel number.

A channel is up while the line is up, both multiplexers are online, and both ends of the channel have a network. It carries up to a Fiber Cable's lanes, less the line's span loss (the line's loss is that of its longest span). The multiplexer looks at its line whenever the networks change, and every two seconds anyway.

States: off, on (its channel lights), or fault when it's paired but the line is down.

The panel (right-click the block) has two tabs: Channels (each channel's network and state; select a channel for the next Link Card, or clear one) and Line (its partner, the line and its spans). Clearing and unpairing need Build.

The Optical Multiplexer (Rack) is the same in a rack: its line is a Fiber Cable leaving the rack at any connection point and leading to its partner (see Rack devices).

Optical Transport Shelf#

For heavier long-haul links, the 6U Optical Transport Shelf does a multiplexer's job at a larger scale, built from cards: Transponder Cards for the channels (1 to 40), Mux Cards to carry more than one channel, Amplifier Cards for extra reach, and Protection Cards for 1+1 protection.

With a Protection Card, the shelf keeps two paths to its partner: a working path (the shortest) and a protect path, which leaves its rack from a different connection point over a different fibre. If the working path is cut, it switches to protect within a tick, and (if the card is set to revertive, the default) back again once the working path is restored. CHGPROT switches by hand for testing.

Everything about the shelf is on Modular chassis.

Watching it from the Terminal OS#

Display Optical (DSPOPT) lists the network's multiplexers and shelves with their spans and channels: length, loss, lanes and quality. RTVOPTSTS reads them into a program. The trigger event *SPANLOSS fires when an optical span loses lanes or gets them back, and *PROTSWITCH when a shelf switches path.
